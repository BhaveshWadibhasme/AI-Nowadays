Open source · Managed Deep Agents

LangChain open-sources its internal design agent

LangChain built Content Studio, a design agent that lives in Slack. Every day, LangChain employees ask it to create videos, social graphics, recreated UI images, slide decks and more. Since launch it has delivered 3,000+ assets, including 80+ slide decks, and the median request costs about 50 cents. It runs on Managed Deep Agents, and the harness is now open source.

Amal Irgashev (GTM Agents at LangChain) describes what's inside: a media harness built on Deep Agents where every Slack thread gets its own sandbox with a video and animation engine, headless Chromium, design tools and brand assets; a design wiki of principles and AI-slop anti-patterns; async subagents that build films and decks in parallel for hours on a shared file system; memory that learns from the team, plus 26 evals to test every change; and Paper and Hyperframes from HeyGen built in. It can be forked, customized and deployed to a company's Slack with one command on Managed Deep Agents.

LangChain's behind-the-scenes write-up covers why they deleted most of their rules when a better model shipped, the “AI slop” patterns they taught it to avoid, how async subagents build in the background while the thread stays open, and how it learns from every finished job.

Why it matters

A rare, real cost figure for a production agent (about 50 cents a request) and an open harness you can fork. It is a ready-made reference design for an internal content or reporting agent in your own department, complete with memory, evals and long-running subagents.

Webinar · GTM Engineering Live

How LangChain taught its design agent taste

LangChain reshared Shreya Talegaonkar (Brand Design at LangChain): “No sharp edges” is a note any designer understands, yet the agent's first version of the graphic came back as a boxy line with hard edges. Brand tokens tell a model which colors and fonts to use, but not what a piece is for, so it fills every open decision with whatever it has seen most.

For months she and Amal Irgashev have been teaching it judgment: every note comes with the reasoning behind it, and each one lands in a skill or an automated check. Amal Irgashev, Daniel Lambert, Jan Franco Glanc Gomez and Shreya walk through it at GTM Engineering Live on Oct 28 at 11 AM PT (11:30 PM IST).

Why it matters

The pattern of turning every piece of feedback into a written rule or an automated check is how you make an agent meet a team’s quality bar, not just a style guide. It applies to any agent producing reports or slides for stakeholders.

Talk · Interrupt London

Harrison Chase on agents that improve over time

At Interrupt London, Harrison Chase demonstrated how organizations can build agents that improve over time and how teams are doing this today. The full stage session shows what it takes to get to an agent that catches its own mistake and writes the eval and the fix.

Why it matters

Self-correcting agents are the next step after basic evals. Watching how the loop from mistake to eval to fix is wired up is useful groundwork for proposing agent improvement processes at work.

Workshop · Today, Oct 28

Agent Evals 101: can you trust an LLM judge?

LangChain Education Engineer Divya Santhanam runs a live virtual workshop on October 28. Topics: how to score a trajectory with the trajectory match evaluator, how to tune matching so evals fail on real behavior changes rather than argument formatting, when to use deterministic matching vs. LLM-as-judge, and how to wire evals into your loop so regressions show up before they ship.

Why it matters

Free, practical training on the measurement pillar. Knowing when an LLM judge can be trusted and when deterministic checks are better is a core skill for anyone signing off on agents in production.

Case study · Interrupt London

Dun & Bradstreet: as AI starts acting, trust becomes infrastructure

LangChain reshared Dun & Bradstreet's recap of a headline fireside chat at Interrupt '26 London, where D&B's Ilya Meyzin joined Harrison Chase. Harrison highlighted D&B as one of the early enterprise adopters of agentic AI, and they discussed D&B's evolution from conversational agents to long-running deep agents that perform complex analytical tasks, and how its governance and observability evolved alongside growing customer adoption.

D&B says its Risk Analytics AI agentic capabilities, delivered via Model Context Protocol, are now available in hyperscaler AI platforms, enterprise data platforms and coding assistants, reducing compliance and third-party risk workflows from days to seconds.

Why it matters

A named enterprise case study with a clear arc from chatbot to deep agents, plus governance and observability growing alongside it. Useful when making the case for agentic AI in a regulated, risk-heavy business.

Trend watch

Today's posts are one story told from three angles: agents that improve themselves. LangChain's design agent learns from every finished job and is checked by 26 evals, Harrison Chase shows an agent that catches its own mistake and writes the eval and the fix, and the Agent Evals 101 workshop teaches how to score whole trajectories so regressions surface before they ship. Dun & Bradstreet adds the enterprise view: as agents start acting rather than answering, governance and observability become the infrastructure of trust. The common thread is that evals are moving from a test step to the engine of the agent's improvement loop.
