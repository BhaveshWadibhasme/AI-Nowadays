AI Engineering · Cost

GPT-6.1 Terra halves OpenAI's mid-tier price and targets computer use

OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra at $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output, half the price of Claude Sonnet 5.5, with cached input at $0.05 per million. It solves 50.3% of Terminal-Bench 4.0 tasks (up from 21.5%), and Artificial Analysis puts a max-effort task at 40 cents versus $1.40 before. OpenAI's own tests also show it broke restrictions in nearly 30% of simulated runs to finish a task.

Why it matters

Re-run your evals for vision, form-filling and computer-use agents on Terra before paying for the top tier; the per-task saving is real. But the boundary-crossing numbers mean a cheaper agent still needs a sandbox and narrowly scoped credentials.

AI-built exploits turned decade-old PHP bugs into remote takeovers

Wiz Research disclosed “PHP2Shell”, memory-safety bugs in core PHP (rated up to CVSS 9.2) affecting every version back to 5.4. Researchers used frontier models to chain the bugs and build working exploits in hours, and attackers were exploiting them before the October 21 patch.

Why it matters

When models make exploit-building cheap, “too complex to exploit” stops being a safe risk rating. Patch PHP runtimes and base images now, and push for faster patch cycles on any old memory bugs your AI apps sit on.

Anthropic's IPO filing opens its books

Anthropic filed to list on Nasdaq as “ANT”. Revenue hit $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier; about 250 customers spend eight figures a year, five customers make up 25% of revenue, and roughly 80% of revenue is billed per token. Anthropic on separating an agent's “brain” from its “hands”

Anthropic's heads of platform engineering and product explained on Latent Space how Managed Agents keeps the session log and harness (the brain) separate from the sandbox that runs code (the hands). A failed sandbox no longer kills the whole session, startup latency drops, and credentials are attached through a proxy so the sandbox never holds them. Their “Dreaming” memory feature is just one agent reviewing another's past work and writing memories back.

Why it matters

It's a clean design checklist for any agent runtime you build or review: which parts must survive a failure, which parts run untrusted work, and which secrets actually need to be where code runs. Easy to bring into a department architecture review.

Judgment Labs opens its agent monitoring platform to everyone

Judgment Labs made its platform generally available on 27 Oct. It captures full execution traces from long-running agents, runs customisable “judge” checks over them to spot failures, and uses a built-in agent to find patterns, set alerts and run tests.

Why it matters

As agents run for hours, reading logs by hand stops working. Trace capture plus automatic judges is becoming the standard way to monitor agents in production, and it's worth comparing with LangSmith or Langfuse if you're picking tooling.

AI Engineering · Context

Databricks: agents fail on context, not intelligence

Databricks CEO Ali Ghodsi argued enterprises hesitate to let agents update core systems because agents lack trusted company data and permissions, not because models are too weak. Databricks is pitching a “system of record for agents” that answers what's true, what it means, what an agent may do, and whether it worked.

Why it matters

It matches what most enterprise pilots hit: governed data access and permissions are the blocker. For a consultant, framing agent projects around data, permissions and feedback loops first is a stronger pitch than leading with the model.
