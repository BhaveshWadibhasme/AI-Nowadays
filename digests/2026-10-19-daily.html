Agents got a reality check over the weekend. A new benchmark shows they still miss most real customer-delivery work, while regulators, outside auditors and open-source sandboxes all moved to put firmer guardrails around them.

Research / From X

Agents fail most real “forward-deployed engineer” tasks (FDE-Gym)

FDE-Gym is a new benchmark of 95 end-to-end delivery tasks from 20 real customer projects across 18 sectors. Agents talk to scripted stakeholders and change production-like systems, and are scored on how much they improve the customer's own success metric. The best of 13 model setups averaged 0.56 and fully resolved only 14 of 95 tasks. The main reason was failing to uncover constraints nobody wrote down, and even when the best setup had every key fact, it hit the bar on only 10 of 23 tasks.

Why it matters

This is the forward-deployed engineer job measured directly. Discovery (asking the right stakeholder questions and surfacing hidden rules) is still where humans add the most value, and it is a good checklist for scoping any agent project.

Frameworks / Tools

Perplexity open-sources SPACE, its agent sandbox

Perplexity released SPACE under Apache 2.0. It runs each agent task in its own small Firecracker virtual machine, keeps credentials and network access controlled from outside the sandbox, and supports pause, resume and branching of sessions. It has powered all of Perplexity's Computer sessions since July, and in 108 escape attempts by models with root access inside the VM, none reached the host.

Why it matters

Letting agents run code is where enterprise security teams push back. This is a production-tested reference design you can point to, or adopt, when an agent needs to execute code or touch files inside corporate IT.

News / Governance

Regulators and outside auditors move closer to the AI labs

The US FTC sent OpenAI and Anthropic more than 50 pages of demands each about agents going rogue and the safeguards meant to stop that, and a source told Axios it plans to have top executives testify. A day later, Anthropic said six evaluators from METR, a nonprofit that tests models for risks, now work inside the company with employee-like access and can publish findings without Anthropic's veto.

Why it matters

Expect customers, auditors and risk teams to ask for agent incident logs, safeguards and independent testing. Building audit trails and clear approval steps into agent projects now will save painful retrofits later.

News / Models

Meta releases Muse Spark Open, the top US open-weight model

Meta released Muse Spark Open, a mixture-of-experts model with 816B total and 42B active parameters, a 1M-token context window, and text, image, video and audio input. Artificial Analysis scores it 39, the best US open-weight model but behind GLM-5.3 (45) and Kimi K3 (44), and it ranks last among the top nine open models on agentic tests. Hosted pricing starts at $1.25 / $4.25 per million input/output tokens. The license requires “Built with Muse Spark Open” attribution and a separate deal for model-hosting businesses with more than $20M in revenue.

Why it matters

For organisations that want US-origin open weights they can host themselves, this is a real option, but check the license terms and test it on your own agent workflows before choosing it over stronger Chinese open models.

News / Enterprise

Claude now writes about 90% of Claude Code, and git couldn't keep up

Anthropic says Claude writes about 90% of Claude Code's own code. Merged pull requests doubled from June to September while the share that had to be reverted fell by more than 40%. Across Anthropic, merges went from about 2,500 a day in May to more than 30,000 a day in September, so a team of seven built a replacement source-control system called Scion.

Why it matters

When AI writes most of the code, the bottleneck moves to review, testing, CI and source control. That is where teams adopting coding agents should invest next.

Frameworks

Frameworks converge on “traces to evals to fine-tuning”

LangChain's October roundup from Interrupt adds LangSmith Fine-Tuning (turn production traces into training data and fine-tune with Baseten or Fireworks), LangSmith Engine v2 with red teaming, a Trajectories view across agents and subagents, Managed Deep Agents v0.9, and FastMCP support in LangChain v1.4. OpenAI's Agents SDK 0.24 adds MCP annotations and pauses automatic context compaction while an approval is pending, and CrewAI 1.15.28 adds a crewai eval command that shows where a run broke.

Why it matters

The frameworks are converging on one loop: trace everything, evaluate it, then improve the model from your own production data. That loop is what an enterprise AI practice should standardise on.

Research / Cost

Two cheaper ways to run long agents: self-edited context and decision models

Meta researchers introduced Context Language Models, where the agent edits a file that becomes its next prompt, cutting failed attempts and stale search results. With training, a Qwen3.5-9B agent went from 28.8% to 42.5% on BrowseComp-Plus, and on TerminalBench 2.1 it matched the strongest baseline with 29.5% less estimated compute. Separately, small “decision models” that return probabilities for choices you define (continue, retry, escalate) are maturing: Nace's self-hostable Drex 2.0 (35B total, about 3B active) scores 61.25 on the public Decision Index versus 57.96 for TypeSafe's Jev 1.13.

Why it matters

Long agent runs get expensive because the context keeps growing and big models make every small decision. Trimming context and sending routine choices to a small, cheap model are two practical cost levers, but test that the agent doesn't delete facts it needs later.
