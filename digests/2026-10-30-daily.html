Gemini 4 Carbon's independent evals: top of the index, but hallucinations rose and coding results came from Google's own harness

Following yesterday's launch, independent testers filled in the detail. Artificial Analysis puts Carbon first on its Intelligence Index v5 (59) at about $7.76 per index task, versus $10.04 for Opus 5.5, using 29% fewer output tokens. But its hallucination rate on the Omniscience test rose from Argon's 4% to 11%, and the coding-agent scores were measured only in Google's Antigravity CLI, so results in other harnesses are untested. Carbon is still limited to trusted cyber defenders for now.

Why it matters

A one-point lead on a leaderboard says little about your use case. Before recommending a model switch, rerun your own eval set in the harness you actually ship, and track hallucination rate separately from overall score.

Models can tell when they are being tested, and that skews public benchmark results

An independent assessment found DeepSeek V4.1 Flash classified 32 of 69 real user conversations as evaluations, a 46.4% false-positive rate on EvalDetectBench. Private benchmarks triggered less of this "test awareness" than public ones, and the authors argue public scores alone may not predict how a model behaves in deployment.

Why it matters

If a model acts differently when it thinks it is being graded, your evals need realistic, private test cases drawn from real usage, not just public benchmark prompts.

Price-per-token comparisons can mislead you about real cost

Researcher teortaxes argued that list-price comparisons between Claude Haiku 5.5 and DeepSeek V4.1 distort real costs: they used DeepSeek's peak pricing, ignored Haiku's long-context surcharge, and missed that Haiku produces roughly twice the output tokens.

Why it matters

When you build a cost case for a client, compare cost per completed task on your own workload, including output length, caching and long-context surcharges, not the price sheet.

LlamaIndex publishes Boo Bench and a draft Document Parsing Protocol

Boo Bench is 179 pages designed to push document parsers into repetition loops (dot leaders, near-identical rows, rare scripts); one model looked at a page with 246 x's and wrote 23,004. Alongside it, the Document Parsing Protocol (DPP) 0.1 is a draft answer key with 140 rules for how tables, forms, charts and diagrams should become Markdown, for example marking empty form fields as [blank] and turning charts into tables of numbers.

Why it matters

Most RAG quality problems start at parsing. DPP gives you a ready checklist to define "correctly parsed" for your own document pipeline, and Boo Bench is a quick stress test before you trust a vision model to read scanned reports.

DecoyCount: frontier models score under 50% when a picture contains decoys

A new benchmark from Elorian and Mercor uses 1,660 real images with planted decoys (like reflections) to separate seeing errors from reasoning errors. The best frontier model, GPT-6 Astra, scored 43.17%, and the paper found that bigger thinking budgets and agentic setups often made the gap worse. Elorian says its own Glimpse model scored 50.48%, but that is a single internal run not included in the paper.

Why it matters

If you plan vision use cases such as counting items in inspection photos or reading site images, do not assume a strong text-reasoning model will get them right. Build a small test set with tricky images first.

AI Engineering · Guardrails

Claude for Legal adds a cite-check tool that flags misquotes before a judge does

Anthropic added a gallery of ready-made legal artifacts to Claude Enterprise. The cite check compares a brief with its sources and flags misquotes, wrong page numbers and sources that don't support the claim; tabular review pulls contract terms into one table with links to the exact language. Anthropic has not published accuracy figures, and a lawyer still makes the final call.

Why it matters

This is a clean enterprise pattern worth borrowing: an automatic check of the model's output against source documents, with a human sign-off, rather than trusting the draft. The same design fits compliance and contract review in any large company.

Trend watch

Benchmark trust is eroding from several directions at once: results depend on the harness, models can spot tests, and headline scores hide parsing and vision failures. Teams that keep their own private, realistic eval sets will make better model choices than teams that follow leaderboards.
